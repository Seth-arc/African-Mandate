# African Mandate: Sahel Arena
### About & Experience Overview

---

## What Is African Mandate?

**African Mandate: Sahel Arena** is a browser-based geopolitical simulation game in which the player assumes the role of an African Union Special Envoy deployed to the Sahel region. The mandate is stabilization. The reality is a compounding cycle of climate collapse, insurgent expansion, military coups, and fractured coalitions.

The game is not a military strategy title. It is a crisis-management simulation where almost every decision carries tradeoffs — between security and legitimacy, between short-term gains and long-term governance, between managing foreign allies and maintaining regional trust. There are no clean solutions. The Sahel's problems resist them.

---

## Setting & Context

The Sahel — the semi-arid band stretching across West and Central Africa — is one of the world's most complex humanitarian and security environments. Desertification is accelerating, reducing arable land and displacing communities. State authority is collapsing in multiple countries simultaneously. Insurgent networks exploit the vacuum left by retreating Western forces. Transitional military juntas govern without democratic mandate.

The game focuses on five countries:

| Country | Capital | Population | Starting Insurgency Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mali | Bamako | 22 million | High (65/100) |
| Burkina Faso | Ouagadougou | 23 million | Moderate-High (60/100) |
| Niger | Niamey | 27 million | Moderate (55/100) |
| Chad | N'Djamena | 18 million | Lower (50/100) |
| Mauritania | Nouakchott | 5 million | Lowest (35/100) |

Within these countries, 30+ distinct zones are tracked — from conflict hotspots and border regions to capitals, urban centres, and remote contested territories. Each zone has its own population, threat level, displaced persons count, ethnic composition, and key actors.

---

## The Mission

The player is appointed AU Special Envoy and given a 48-month mandate (20 turns) to stabilise the region. The campaign is structured across **five acts**, each of roughly four turns, following a narrative arc from initial assessment through coalition-building, military adaptation, strategic reckoning, and final resolution.

**Five key metrics** define the health of the mission:

- **Stability** — Is governance functioning? Is the region moving toward order or collapse?
- **Insurgency Level** — How active, entrenched, and expanding are non-state armed groups?
- **Civilian Support** — Do the 11 million affected civilians trust the mission?
- **Global Legitimacy** — Is the international community backing the mandate or withdrawing?
- **Regional Synergy** — Are partner nations and regional blocs cooperating or fragmenting?

These are not independent gauges. Aggressive military operations can reduce insurgency but damage civilian support and global legitimacy. Prioritising governance and accountability builds long-term legitimacy but leaves security gains lagging. Every allocation is a statement about what you are willing to sacrifice.

## Gameplay Structure

### Turns & Actions

Each turn represents two to three months of real time. The player has **three action slots per turn** to deploy across any combination of domains:

- **Security** — Patrols, targeted operations, border reinforcement
- **Diplomacy** — Junta engagement, ECOWAS coordination, international outreach, civil society partnership
- **Governance** — Anti-corruption monitoring, capacity building, integrity frameworks
- **Humanitarian** — Aid distribution, safe corridor establishment, IDP camp support
- **Intelligence** — Network cultivation, source verification, threat assessment
- **Community** — Local mediation, splinter faction negotiation
- **Climate & Resources** — Drought resilience, water resource management

Actions consume **resources**: a limited budget (starting at $15M), political capital, personnel headcount, intel points, and operational time. None of these replenish easily. Every action has minimum and maximum cost parameters, allowing the player to calibrate investment — but higher investment is not always better if it leaves other areas underfunded.

### The Resource Economy

| Resource | Starting Value | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Budget | $15M | Hard currency for all operational activities |
| Political Capital | 60 | Diplomatic and institutional credibility |
| Personnel | 2,000 | Deployable human capacity |
| Intel Points | 20 | Access to gated intelligence-dependent actions |
| Time (Months) | 48 | Consumed by longer operations; cannot be recovered |

Some actions require minimum intel thresholds before they become available. Others impose cooldowns of one to three turns. Certain high-risk operations — targeted strikes, for instance — carry a 15% chance of civilian harm, with direct consequences for civilian support and global legitimacy metrics.

### Events & Crises

The game generates events dynamically based on the state of the mission. If stability falls below 45 for two consecutive turns, a governance crisis triggers. If the player has not established oversight mechanisms, a corruption scandal may surface. If insurgency surges in a specific territory, an intercommunal violence event may escalate.

Event types span:
- **Security**: Insurgent counter-tactics, activity surges, unrest spikes
- **Corruption & Governance**: Procurement fraud, embezzlement, audit failures, media scandals
- **Coalition & Geopolitical**: Partner defections, AES consolidation, ECOWAS alienation, Wagner Group presence
- **Climate & Humanitarian**: Drought crises, displacement surges, food insecurity
- **Intelligence**: Source activations, intel confidence degradation

Unresolved events impose penalty bundles. The player cannot address everything. Choosing which crises to absorb and which to prevent is the core skill of the game.

## Key Actors

The player manages relationships with **16 tracked individuals, factions, and institutions**. Relationships shift based on actions taken, decisions made, and events that unfold.

**African Union Leadership**
- **Dr. Amara Diallo** — AU Chairperson, mission authority and narrative anchor
- **Ambassador Fatou Bensouda** — Peace and Security Council, diplomatic architecture
- **Lt. Gen. Amina Konte** — Force Commander, military operations
- **Dr. Kofi Mensah** — Governance Advisor, accountability and anti-corruption

**State & Regional Actors**
- **General Ibrahim Traoré** — Burkina Faso Junta Leader; sovereignty-focused, resistant to external oversight
- **Mali & Niger Transitional Governments** — Military administrations requiring delicate engagement
- **ECOWAS** — West African Economic Community; democratic governance advocate with sanctions leverage
- **AES (Alliance of Sahel States)** — Junta-led regional alternative; suspicious of Western-aligned institutions

**Civil Society & Community**
- **Aïssata Konaté** — Sahel Civil Society Coalition leader; community-mediation advocate; highest baseline trust (68/100)
- **Dogon & Fulani Communities** — Ethnic groups in multi-ethnic zones, central to intercommunal conflict dynamics

**External & Adversarial**
- **Insurgent Networks** — Adaptive, AI-driven, non-negotiable
- **Insurgent Splinter Faction** — Conditionally negotiable breakaway group (requires specific unlocks)
- **Wagner Group** — PMC presence that escalates as a consequence of certain failure states
- **International Donors** — Budget conditionality tied to legitimacy metrics
- **Media & Oversight Networks** — External accountability triggers for corruption events

Actors can be engaged through **dialogue systems** — multi-choice conversations that shift relationship scores and unlock new action options. Dialogue with junta leaders forces the player to navigate between respecting sovereignty and applying leverage. Civil society conversations challenge a purely military framing of the mission.

## Strategic Paths

There is no single correct approach. Five broad strategic orientations exist:

**1. Security-First**
Prioritise patrols, targeted operations, and border reinforcement. Produces the fastest insurgency reductions but risks civilian harm, damages global legitimacy, and creates coalition friction with ECOWAS.

**2. Governance-Focused**
Invest in institutions, oversight, and anti-corruption mechanisms. Slow initial security gains, but builds the legitimacy required for durable stability. Resource-intensive in political capital and budget.

**3. Coalition-Building**
Engage all factions and maximise relationship capital. Highly flexible but requires careful balance between ECOWAS democratic norms and AES junta pragmatism — they are frequently in opposition.

**4. Community-Led**
Partner with civil society, emphasise local mediation over military action. Builds the highest civilian support but may alienate state actors and moves more slowly against active insurgencies.

**5. Pragmatic Balance**
Mix approaches as the situation demands. The highest complexity path, with the most branching narrative outcomes and the greatest demand on resource management.

## Endings

The campaign concludes at Turn 20. Five distinct endings are possible, each with unique narrative narration:

| Outcome | Condition |
|---|---|
| **Strategic Success** | All five win-condition thresholds met; no critical zones persist at endgame |
| **Fragile Success** | Thresholds met, but late-phase risk indicators signal precarious stability |
| **Stalemate** | Mixed results; catastrophe avoided but transformation not achieved |
| **Regional Setback** | Too many thresholds missed; insurgent networks have exploited coalition fractures |
| **Mandate Revoked** | Catastrophic failure; any metric in the critical band for three consecutive turns |

Win thresholds:
- Stability ≥ 55
- Insurgency ≤ 45
- Civilian Support ≥ 50
- Global Legitimacy ≥ 55
- Regional Synergy ≥ 55

Starting values are below every win threshold. Every metric requires active investment to move.

## User Experience

### Landing & Entry

The game begins with a **cinematic landing page** — a slow-burn scroll through five narrative sections, each framing a dimension of the crisis: the situation on the ground, the players, the stakes, the limits of force, and the mission itself. A parallax hero image — depicting Sahel figures against an arid landscape — pans and zooms as the user scrolls, synchronized to each narrative beat via GSAP ScrollTrigger animation.

On reaching the final section, the player can click **Play** to enter the arena or **About** to view the mission briefing modal.

Clicking Play initiates an **intro video sequence** — an opening cinematic that transitions the interface from the landing page into the game. A brief "Initializing mission..." loading state bridges the cutscene to the game interface.

### Game Interface

The main game interface is divided into three columns:

- **Left sidebar**: Turn progress tracker, resource bars, and metric dials with critical threshold indicators
- **Center**: An interactive Leaflet map (CartoDB Dark Matter basemap) with clickable territory polygons and zone markers, overlaid with a scenario panel for territory and zone detail
- **Right sidebar**: Intel feed (categorised reports with urgency and confidence levels) and actor panel (key tracked figures, relationship scores, dialogue availability)

An **action bar** at the bottom provides the primary interaction controls: configuring and executing actions (a three-step flow: configure → review → outcome), and advancing the turn.

Nineteen distinct modal types handle everything from mission briefing and act transitions to actor dialogues, dossier articles, zone detail views, intel reports, a relationship matrix, campaign status summaries, and the final outcome cinematic.

### Accessibility & Sessions

The game supports **authenticated play** (Google OAuth via Supabase) and **guest play**. Authenticated players benefit from persistent session saves with cloud-backed autosave. Guest sessions are scoped to the current browser session.

## Design Philosophy

African Mandate was designed around a central tension: **the asymmetry between problem complexity and solution capacity**. The Sahel crisis is not solvable within a 48-month mandate. The simulation is not asking the player to solve it. It is asking what kind of failure is acceptable — and what kind of partial success is worth fighting for.

This shapes several deliberate design decisions:

- **Resources are always scarce.** The budget and personnel numbers are calibrated to create genuine tradeoffs, not optimization puzzles.
- **Legitimacy is a resource.** Actions that would be militarily efficient carry diplomatic costs. The game refuses to let security and politics be treated separately.
- **Actors have agency.** The AI director adjusts opposition pressure in response to player strategies. Insurgent networks are not static targets.
- **Context is not decorative.** The five acts, the dialogue system, the cutscenes, and the dossier articles exist to make the scenario legible — not as flavour text around a spreadsheet, but as material that changes what decisions feel like.

The intent is to place the player in a position of genuine responsibility for a region they may know little about, and leave them — regardless of outcome — with a more considered sense of what stabilisation actually demands.
